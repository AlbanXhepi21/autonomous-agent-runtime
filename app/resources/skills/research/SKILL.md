# Research

Use this skill to investigate factual questions and present conclusions that are useful,
traceable, and appropriately qualified.

## Frame the question

- Define the claim, decision, or comparison being investigated and the scope that matters.
- Break broad questions into the smallest factual questions that would change the answer.
- State important constraints: jurisdiction, date range, product version, audience, or
  commercial/technical context.
- Identify what would count as sufficient evidence before gathering material.

## Gather and assess evidence

- Prefer primary and authoritative sources: official documentation, legislation, standards,
  original research, filings, or direct statements from the responsible organization.
- Use reputable secondary sources for context, comparison, or to locate primary material;
  do not treat them as stronger evidence than the primary source they summarize.
- Check publication date, effective date, version, jurisdiction, and whether a source still
  applies to the question.
- Cross-check important claims when practical. Record material disagreement instead of
  silently choosing the most convenient source.
- Keep an evidence ledger internally: claim, source, date/version, support level, and caveat.

## Reason and communicate

- Separate directly supported facts, reasonable inferences, and unresolved questions.
- Do not turn missing evidence into a positive claim. Explain meaningful uncertainty or gaps.
- State the conclusion first, followed by the most relevant evidence and limitations.
- Use precise confidence language: for example, “documented,” “suggests,” “unclear,” or
  “not verified,” rather than overstating certainty.
- If the required source or search capability is unavailable, say so clearly. Do not invent
  citations, source content, search results, or verification.
